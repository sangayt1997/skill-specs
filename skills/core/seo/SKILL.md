# SEO

Improve a public page's ability to be discovered, understood, selected, and used without sacrificing accuracy or the visitor's task. Treat SEO as an evidence-driven product discipline spanning content, site architecture, and technical delivery—not as keyword insertion or a promise of rankings.

Use the target search engine, market, language, device mix, site type, and business outcome named by the user. If they are unstated, make only reversible assumptions and label them. Current search results, platform documentation, site analytics, and crawl data are time-sensitive; verify them when the task depends on them.

## Scope and Activation

Apply this skill to SEO research, content briefs and drafts, metadata, structured data, crawlability, indexation, canonicalization, internal linking, search-performance analysis, and technical or content audits. Do not activate it for ordinary copywriting, private documentation, or unrelated frontend work unless the user explicitly requests an SEO outcome.

Select only the guidance relevant to the task: content and intent, technical signals, or audit and measurement. Ordinary SEO work does not automatically authorize publishing, changing production configuration, submitting URLs, crawling a production site aggressively, or modifying analytics accounts.

## Working Method

1. Define the page's audience, job, funnel role, conversion or satisfaction outcome, locale, and constraints.
2. Establish evidence. Inspect the page, rendered output, internal links, templates, first-party data, and current search results that are actually available. Record missing access rather than inventing findings.
3. Identify the dominant search need and the page type capable of satisfying it. Consider informational, navigational, commercial-investigation, transactional, local, and mixed needs without forcing every query into one label.
4. Diagnose the limiting layer in order: discovery, crawl access, rendering, indexability, canonicalization, search presentation, task satisfaction, and measurement. Do not rewrite content when a technical blocker explains the problem.
5. Produce the smallest deliverable that solves the request: a brief, draft, metadata set, markup, implementation, or prioritized audit.
6. Validate facts, rendered behavior, machine-readable signals, and measurement. Separate what was observed from what was inferred or recommended.

## Non-Negotiable Guardrails

- **MUST** optimize for the visitor's task and accurately represent the page, organization, author, product, evidence, and availability.
- **MUST** distinguish verified search demand, search-result observations, analytics, and crawl evidence from hypotheses.
- **MUST NOT** invent search volume, rankings, customer experience, expert review, tests, quotations, statistics, credentials, prices, or product capabilities.
- **MUST NOT** fabricate originality to satisfy an "information gain" requirement. Seek first-party evidence, expert input, original analysis, examples, or data; identify the gap when none is available.
- **MUST NOT** stuff keywords, create doorway pages, spin near-duplicate pages, hide text or links, misuse structured data, or recommend scaled low-value publishing.
- **MUST NOT** guarantee indexing, rich results, featured snippets, traffic, conversions, or ranking positions.
- **MUST NOT** present E-E-A-T as a single score, tag, schema type, or direct ranking factor. Use it as a trust-oriented self-assessment lens, with greater care for health, safety, financial, civic, or other high-impact topics.
- **SHOULD** preserve the user's voice, factual claims, legal constraints, and established information architecture unless changing them is in scope.
- **SHOULD** prefer primary sources and first-party evidence for factual or time-sensitive claims.

## Evidence and Research

Do not infer a live search landscape from general knowledge. When the user asks for keyword, competitor, or search-result research, capture at least:

- engine, country or region, language, device or personalization limitations, query, and observation date;
- dominant result types and recurring user needs;
- credible content gaps the site is qualified to fill; and
- whether volume, difficulty, clicks, conversions, or rankings came from an actual named data source.

Search-result similarity is evidence of a common format, not a requirement to imitate every result. Use competitor pages to understand expectations and omissions, never to paraphrase a composite article. For consequential claims, inspect the underlying primary source rather than citing a search snippet.

If browsing or proprietary SEO data is unavailable, create a hypothesis-led plan and clearly list what must be verified. Do not attach precise metrics to intuition.

## Content Quality

Answer the core question as early as the reader needs it, then supply evidence, constraints, procedure, comparisons, and next actions in a logical sequence. A short direct answer can improve usability, but its placement does not make a page eligible for a featured snippet.

Use descriptive headings that reflect document structure. Keep one clear page topic and primary visible heading as a strong editorial default, but do not label a harmless heading-level skip or multiple `h1` elements as a ranking penalty without evidence. Fix heading structure for semantics, navigation, and comprehension.

Cover the vocabulary and entities needed to answer the topic naturally. Do not enforce keyword-density percentages or mechanical exact-match counts. Repetition becomes a defect when it harms clarity, reads unnaturally, or exists mainly to manipulate search systems.

Original value must be real and relevant: first-hand experience, expert explanation, tested procedure, original data, calculations, decision criteria, examples, visuals, or synthesis that helps the reader act. More words are not inherently more complete; stop when the task is satisfied.

### Establish Intent and Page Ownership

Start with the visitor's job, not a keyword string. A query may ask to learn, reach a known entity, compare options, transact, find a local provider, or complete a mixed sequence. Infer the dominant need from query language, first-party Search Console or site-search data, customer research, and current results in the target locale. Record ambiguity instead of assigning intent by formula.

Choose a page type that can satisfy the need, such as a concise answer, guide, category, comparison, tool, product page, or location page. Do not create a URL when an existing page already owns the need. Decide whether to update, consolidate, redirect, canonicalize, or create based on overlap, internal links, user journeys, and technical constraints.

### Build Content Briefs

A useful brief resolves decisions rather than accumulating terms. Include what is available and relevant:

- audience, context, prior knowledge, and desired outcome;
- primary and secondary search needs, page purpose, and page type;
- factual claims and authoritative sources for consequential claims;
- questions, objections, comparisons, entities, attributes, and terminology to cover;
- first-party expertise, examples, measurements, assets, or expert input;
- desired next action and useful internal-link destinations;
- freshness, review, authorship, regulatory, and localization requirements; and
- baseline and success metrics.

Do not derive a required word count from competitor averages. Use a length bound only when the format, editorial system, or user requires one.

### Map Terms and Entities

Group language by the primary concept, required entities and attributes, natural variants, supporting questions, and nearby meanings that belong on another page. Use the map to find missing concepts, ambiguity, page overlap, and cannibalization—not to create quotas. Exact-match repetition, semantic variants, and entity counts are not substitutes for accurate coverage.

### Create and Verify Original Value

Seek first-hand observations, a documented experiment, original data with methodology, expert reasoning, worked examples, calculations, product-specific evidence, or a clearer synthesis of primary sources for a defined audience. If a draft requires an expert quote, test result, proprietary fact, or customer experience that is unavailable, expose the missing input instead of generating plausible content.

Attribute evidence close to consequential claims. Make limitations, dates, risks, commercial relationships, eligibility, availability, and price basis easy to find. Do not add a biography, citations, or author schema solely as a ranking ritual.

### Draft for Task Completion

- Confirm relevance quickly and put the direct answer or next step before background when appropriate.
- Organize sections around reader decisions and questions, not keyword variants.
- Use tables for genuine comparisons, ordered lists for sequences, and prose for reasoning.
- Link internally where another page advances the task, using anchors that describe the destination.
- Write alternative text for an image's purpose, not to insert keywords.
- Use a proportionate next action rather than forcing a sales call to action onto informational content.

Avoid generic scene-setting, fake quotations, unsupported superlatives, copied autocomplete FAQs, repetitive summaries, and sections included only because competitors have them.

## Search-Facing Signals

Keep the visible title, `<title>`, description, canonical URL, social preview, structured data, and page content mutually consistent. Treat title and description lengths as editorial preview budgets when useful, not universal validity boundaries: search engines may rewrite or truncate them according to query, device, language, and presentation.

Add structured data only for content that exists on the page and a supported feature or consumer. Valid markup enables eligibility; it does not guarantee a rich result or higher ranking. Keep social metadata conceptually separate from search metadata even when values are shared.

Robots controls crawling, indexing directives, canonical hints, redirects, HTTP status, and sitemap inclusion solve different problems. Do not substitute one for another. In particular, `robots.txt` is not access control and is not a reliable way to remove a URL from an index.

### Inspect the Delivered Page

Evaluate the final URL, redirect chain, HTTP status, response headers, initial HTML, rendered DOM, essential content, crawlable links, mobile output, blocking resources, canonical, robots directives, language annotations, and template variants. Source HTML alone is insufficient for a JavaScript-rendered page; the browser DOM alone can hide server, header, or initial-response faults. When access exists, compare browser findings with the target search engine's URL inspection or rendering tools.

### Titles and Descriptions

Every indexable page should have a unique, descriptive `<title>` aligned with the visible title and content. Put distinguishing information early, include brand text only when useful, and avoid repeated terms or boilerplate. Write a unique meta description that accurately summarizes the page and provides a relevant reason to visit.

There is no universal character-limit validity rule for either element. If a project requests a preview budget such as 50–60 characters for titles or 150–160 for descriptions, treat it as a heuristic, report the count, and do not mark accurate text invalid solely for crossing it. Search engines may generate query-specific snippets, rewrite titles, or truncate them according to device, language, and presentation. Google does not use the `keywords` meta tag; do not generate it for Google SEO.

### Status, Redirects, and Canonicals

- Return `200` for a successfully served page; use meaningful `3xx`, `4xx`, and `5xx` responses for moves, missing resources, access failures, and server failures. Control indexability separately.
- Use a permanent redirect when a URL has moved and visitors should use the destination.
- Use `rel="canonical"` for duplicate or substantially similar pages that must remain accessible. Prefer an absolute, indexable, equivalent destination.
- Treat canonical declarations as preferences or signals, not guaranteed commands.
- Keep redirects, canonicals, internal links, sitemap URLs, and `hreflang` annotations consistent.
- Do not use `robots.txt`, removal tools, or conflicting `noindex` directives as canonicalization methods.
- Prevent soft 404s: missing entities and error messages must not masquerade as successful content pages.

### Crawling, Indexing, and Sitemaps

Use `robots.txt` to control crawler access, authentication to protect private content, and a supported `noindex` directive on a crawlable response when removal from search is intended. A robots rule can leave a known URL visible without content and must never be treated as access control.

Generate sitemaps from preferred canonical, indexable URLs. Use absolute URLs, valid encoding, accurate substantive modification dates, and the target engine's size constraints. A sitemap aids discovery and supplies hints; it does not guarantee indexing or improve rankings by itself.

Ensure important pages are reachable through real `<a href>` links and a comprehensible site hierarchy. Do not depend only on form submissions, script event handlers, or a sitemap for discovery.

### Structured Data

Choose markup for a feature and consumer the visible page genuinely supports. The markup must describe the primary entity accurately, include required properties and valid values, use canonical resolvable URLs, and match visible facts such as prices, availability, ratings, authorship, and dates. Never mark up hidden or nonexistent content, fake ratings, self-serving reviews, or a more specific type than the evidence supports.

Schema.org validation checks vocabulary and syntax; a search engine's rich-results test checks feature requirements. Neither guarantees display or ranking. Test rendered markup and monitor relevant enhancement reports after release when available.

### Social and International Metadata

Open Graph and platform-specific card metadata control social previews, not ordinary search ranking. Use the target consumer's required properties, stable canonical URLs, accurate page-specific text, correct object types, and accessible representative images. Validate previews with the relevant platform and account for caching and image crops.

For language or regional variants that need separate indexing, use stable URLs, same-language canonicals, reciprocal `hreflang` annotations, and an appropriate fallback where supported. Do not force redirects that prevent users or crawlers from selecting another language. Publish local landing pages only for real locations or service areas with accurate, differentiated information; do not mass-produce city pages with token substitutions.

## Audits and Measurement

Define the property, hostnames, environment, date range, markets, search engines, page types, business outcomes, crawl scope, and access available. Do not imply complete coverage from a sample; disclose URL sources, exclusions, authentication limits, rendering mode, user agent, and data-source limitations.

Prefer evidence in this order when applicable:

1. Live HTTP responses, rendered pages, templates, logs, and configuration.
2. Search-engine webmaster tools for queries, clicks, impressions, indexing, canonicals, and enhancements.
3. Analytics and conversion systems for visitor behavior and outcomes.
4. Crawl datasets and validators for reproducible site-wide patterns.
5. Current search-result observations for presentation and result-type context.
6. Third-party estimates for directional discovery, labeled with provider and limitations.

Do not treat third-party traffic, search volume, authority, or difficulty scores as ground truth. Search Console and analytics measure different stages and may legitimately disagree.

### Diagnose in Dependency Order

Check availability, discovery and crawl, rendering, indexability and canonicalization, search presentation, content and architecture, then experience and business outcomes. A validator warning is not automatically an SEO defect; show how the condition affects discovery, comprehension, eligibility, visitors, or business results.

For a traffic change, verify tracking and reporting first. Compare equal and year-over-year periods, segment by query, page, country, device, search type, and appearance, and distinguish impressions, clicks, click-through rate, position, sessions, and conversions. Check releases, migrations, redirects, canonicals, robots directives, outages, inventory, demand shifts, SERP changes, and representative affected and unaffected URLs before attributing the change to an algorithm update. Temporal coincidence does not establish causation.

### Prioritize Findings

Use scope, severity, user and business impact, evidence strength, confidence, effort, dependencies, reversibility, regression risk, and time sensitivity. Reserve `critical` for evidenced broad failures such as accidental blocking, destructive redirects or canonicals, widespread server failure, or policy and security exposure. A missing optional tag, title-length heuristic, or minor schema suggestion is not critical.

Group repeated template defects into one root-cause finding with representative URLs and estimated reach. Separate confirmed defects, likely issues, hypotheses, and opportunities. Each recommendation should identify the exact condition, expected mechanism, owner, dependencies, acceptance check, rollback consideration, metric, and evaluation window.

### Measure Outcomes

Choose metrics based on the page's role: indexed canonical coverage, crawl or rendering errors, impressions, search appearances, clicks, click-through rate interpreted with position and layout, qualified engagement, task completion, leads, sales, subscriptions, support deflection, corrections, complaints, or template error rates.

Record the baseline, release date, comparison method, segmentation, confounders, and minimum observation window. Rankings are volatile and personalized; do not use a single rank as the sole success measure. Verify deployed output, allow realistic crawl and indexing time, and do not imply that repeated recrawl requests guarantee faster inclusion.

## Output Contract

Match the response to the task. For an audit, report:

```text
Finding:
- Evidence and affected URL/template:
- User or search impact:
- Confidence: confirmed | likely | hypothesis
- Recommendation and owner:
- Priority: critical | high | medium | low
- Verification:
```

For a content brief or draft, include the audience, primary task, page type, evidence inputs, differentiating value, required claims and sources, structure, internal-link opportunities, and conversion or next action. Include keyword or entity lists only when they change coverage decisions.

For metadata or code, provide implementation-ready values and identify dynamic fields, escaping, fallbacks, canonical behavior, and validation steps. Do not add optional tags merely to make the output look comprehensive.

## Completion Checklist

1. Confirm the deliverable matches a real user need and appropriate page type.
2. Verify important claims and disclose missing first-party or live-search evidence.
3. When a page or implementation is in scope, check that essential content is accessible in rendered output and links resolve to intended URLs.
4. Confirm title, description, canonical, robots directives, status code, and structured data do not contradict one another.
5. Test structured data and social previews with the relevant consumer when applicable.
6. Record baseline and outcome metrics appropriate to the page rather than relying on rank alone.
7. Report limitations, hypotheses, and any production actions still requiring authorization.
